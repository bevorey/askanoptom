Your pupillary distance — the gap in mm between your pupils. Labs need it to centre each lens correctly so your vision stays clear and comfortable. Ask AI for more detail →
Short-sightedness. You see clearly up close but distant objects appear blurry. The eye is slightly too long, so light focuses in front of the retina instead of on it. Ask AI for more detail →
Long-sightedness. The eye is slightly too short, so light focuses behind the retina. Often causes eyestrain and headaches — especially with reading — even if distance vision seems fine. Ask AI for more detail →
The cornea or lens is slightly irregular in shape — more rugby ball than football — causing light to focus at multiple points and vision to appear blurred at all distances. Ask AI for more detail →
The natural age-related loss of near focus, typically starting around 40. The lens inside the eye gradually stiffens, making it harder to read up close — hence reading glasses or progressives. Ask AI for more detail →
A lens with no visible line that corrects distance, intermediate, and near vision all at once. Typically prescribed for people over 40 who need correction at multiple distances. Ask AI for more detail →
The most common lens type — one focal power throughout, correcting either distance or near vision but not both. Suits most patients under 40. Ask AI for more detail →
