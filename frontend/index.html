A refraction is the test where your optometrist determines your glasses or contact lens prescription — the "which is better, 1 or 2?" part of your eye exam. It measures how light bends as it enters your eye to find your optimal correction.

Ask AI for more detail →

Your PD is the distance in millimetres between the centres of your pupils. It's essential for making glasses — it tells the lab exactly where to position the optical centre of each lens so your vision is clear and comfortable.

Ask AI for more detail →

Myopia (short-sightedness) means you can see things clearly up close but distant objects appear blurry. It occurs when the eye is slightly too long, causing light to focus in front of the retina rather than on it.

Ask AI for more detail →

Hyperopia (long-sightedness) means the eye is slightly too short, causing light to focus behind the retina. Mild hyperopia can be compensated by the eye's own focusing system, but it often causes eyestrain and headaches — especially with reading.

Ask AI for more detail →

Astigmatism occurs when the cornea or lens is slightly irregular in shape — more like a rugby ball than a football. This causes light to focus at multiple points, resulting in blurred or distorted vision at all distances.

Ask AI for more detail →

Presbyopia is the natural age-related loss of near focusing ability, typically starting around age 40. The lens inside the eye gradually loses flexibility, making it harder to focus on close objects — hence the need for reading glasses or progressives.

Ask AI for more detail →

A progressive (or multifocal) lens has no visible line and provides correction for distance, intermediate, and near vision all in one lens. Typically prescribed for people over 40 who need correction at multiple distances.

Ask AI for more detail →

A single vision lens has one focal power throughout — correcting either distance or near vision, but not both. The most common lens type, suitable for most patients under 40.

Ask AI for more detail →

Dry eye occurs when your eyes don't produce enough tears or the tears evaporate too quickly. Common causes include screen time, air conditioning, contact lens wear, certain medications, and age-related changes in tear production.

Ask AI for more detail →

Eye strain, uncorrected refractive errors, and binocular vision problems can all cause headaches — particularly around the temples or forehead after prolonged screen or reading time. An optometrist can determine if your eyes are the cause.
